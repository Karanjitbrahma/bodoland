From Kokrajhar Weaves to Global Exports: How Aaroki Silks Scaled to ₹2.4 Cr Annual Revenue

Founded by 26-year-old Anusuiya Brahma, the sustainable handloom enterprise connects over 140 home-weavers to ethical fashion retail across metropolitan hubs and European boutiques.

Rishabh Narzary • Senior Enterprise Reporter

The Problem: Trapped in Middleman Margins

Deep in the lush sal timber belts of Kokrajhar, almost every rural household maintains an ancestral throw-shuttle loom. For generations, Bodo women have produced Eri silk—a rare cruelty-free, thermally regulating wild fiber celebrated worldwide as Ahimsa silk. Yet until 2023, nearly all economic surplus escaped the region entirely.

Local aggregators purchased unfinished stoles from home artisans at an average price of ₹450 per unit. Those identical weaves resurfaced in high-end design boutiques of South Delhi and Khan Market priced upwards of ₹7,500. The creators themselves remained vulnerable to seasonal debt cycles, unable to forecast yarn expenditures or reinvest in improved machinery.

Armed with a degree in textile development from NIFT Kolkata and seed capital of ₹3.5 Lakhs from the Bodoland Youth Entrepreneurship Grant, Anusuiya returned to her native Dotoma block with a single operational thesis: decouple the craftswomen from traditional aggregators through transparent digital infrastructure.

Building the Distributed Weaving Cluster

The primary operational hurdle wasn't demand; it was synchronization. Kokrajhar and Chirang experience prolonged monsoon deluges between May and September, which frequently severed physical contact with remote weaving enclaves like Kachugaon and Bijni.

Rather than attempting to construct a centralized, capital-intensive factory floor—which would have torn women away from their agricultural routines and child-rearing duties—Aaroki created eight autonomous micro-hubs. Each hub is managed by a literate lead weaver equipped with an LTE tablet and barcode labeling thermal printers.

inventory_2

Decentralized Inventory Architecture

By grouping inventory via WhatsApp Business catalog webhooks, each village cluster broadcasts finished piece certifications directly to the central Guwahati outbound fulfillment center, triggering instant advance UPI milestone payments into weaver bank accounts.

In late 2024, Aaroki caught the eye of an ethical textile procurement syndicate based in Hamburg, Germany. The syndicate ordered an experimental batch of 400 vegetable-dyed Eri shawls, certifying zero chemical residues. That single pilot opened doors to five specialty concept stores in Berlin, Zurich, and Amsterdam, turning what was once a hyper-local handicraft into an export-grade luxury offering.

lightbulb What Northeast Founders Can Learn

1. Traceability as a Pricing Moat

Global consumers willingly pay a 3x premium when each garment comes with a QR passport identifying the artisan, date spun, and village GPS coordinates.

2. Standardize Packaging Locally

Eliminate transit damages before air freight by switching to moisture-barrier bio-compostable cassava bags designed for humid sub-Himalayan monsoon transit.

3. Bypass Wholesale Consignment

Refuse non-refundable 90-day retail credit schemes. Direct boutique partnerships with 50% upfront deposits protect small batch artisan working capital.

4. Build for Digital Micro-Payments

Same-day UPI settlements built radical trust among first-time women entrepreneurs, cutting seasonal attrition from 40% to under 4%.
